How to translate a painting recipe between paint brands

Translate what each paint does before you translate what it is called. A colour match can suggest candidates; a reliable recipe also preserves opacity, finish, value order, and application.

By Bas MahieuPublished 2 August 2026Updated 2 August 20269 minute read
Short answerWrite down the role, product type, finish, and undercoat for every paint in the original recipe. Find same-role candidates with the closest recorded colour, treating ΔE ≤5 as a shortlist. Test the translated sequence instead of each bottle in isolation because opacity and layering change the final result.

1. Inventory the original recipe

Begin with the exact identity of every product: brand, range, paint name, code, and whether it is a base, layer, wash, glaze, metallic, transparent one-coat paint, ink, or effect product. Repeated names and reformulations make the range and code important.

Then record the part a colour plays. “Red” is not a role. “Opaque dark-red base over black primer” and “thin warm-red glaze over the midtone” are roles. Those two steps should not receive the same substitute even if their visible swatches are similar.

2. Separate hard constraints from preferences

A hard constraint is something the replacement must do: cover in two coats, flow into recesses, stay metallic, or remain translucent over the previous layer. A preference is something you can adapt: exact brand, bottle format, or a tiny colour difference that the next layer will cover.

This ordering prevents the most common failure in automated conversion tables: selecting the numerically closest colour even though it is the wrong material for the step.

3. Build a colour shortlist

Use the BrushForge converter to compare the original with a target brand. Start with candidates in the same behaviour family. ΔE2000 indicates distance between stored catalogue colours; lower is closer. Use ≤5 as a useful shortlist when possible, not a guarantee.

If the nearest same-type candidate is above 10, write “different colour” in the translation rather than silently presenting it as equivalent. At that point it may be better to mix, retain the original brand for one step, or adjust the surrounding highlight and shadow.

4. Check the value sequence

A recipe is a relationship between steps. Lay the original and proposed base, midtone, and highlight swatches in order. If the replacement midtone is too light, the highlight may no longer read. If the wash is more opaque, it may collapse the separation created underneath.

Translate the complete local sequence before buying. One slightly warmer paint can work when the rest of the ladder follows it; a single “closer” replacement can fail if it breaks value progression.

Original workflow insightStore a purpose with every paint line in BrushForge: “armour base,” “leather recess,” or “final edge.” When a paint is discontinued later, the role survives even if the product does not.

5. Make a controlled test

  1. Prime a spare part, base rim, or plastic card with the intended undercoat.
  2. Apply the same thinning and number of coats used in the original recipe.
  3. Let each layer dry before judging colour and sheen.
  4. Apply the next recipe step; do not judge a midtone only in isolation.
  5. Compare under neutral desk lighting and the display lighting you normally use.
  6. Add the intended varnish before making a final decision if it changes the finish.

Washes, metallics, and transparent one-coat paints

These require stricter role matching. A wash is shaped by flow, surface tension, and pigment load. A metallic is shaped by particle reflectance and orientation. Contrast-, Speedpaint-, and Xpress-style products use transparency and pooling to build local value variation. A flat screen swatch cannot encode those behaviours.

As of the 10 July 2026 snapshot, BrushForge separates translucent products into 301 single-coat, 148 wash, 42 ink, 10 glaze, and 12 other translucent records. Compatible matches stay inside the exact family; another translucent family can be shown only as a warned reference.

6. Save the translated recipe

Keep the original and translated versions side by side. Record the date, target brand, rejected candidates, and the physical test result. If a paint worked only with an extra coat or a different dilution, that adjustment is part of the translated recipe.
